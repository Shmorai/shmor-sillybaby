Hunger cues: feed early, feed calm

Babies show hunger long before they cry. Responding to early cues makes feeding calmer for both of you, whether you breastfeed or bottle feed.

1. Early: “I’m hungry”

Stirring, opening the mouth, turning the head, rooting (searching for the breast), sucking on hands or lips.

2. Active: “I’m really hungry”

Stretching, wriggling more, bringing hands to the mouth, fussing.

3. Late: “Calm me, then feed me”

Crying, going red, agitated movements. Settle your baby first (cuddle, skin to skin), then feed.

Fullness cues include turning away, closing the mouth, slowing down and relaxed, open hands.

Feeding at a glance, by age

  • 0–6 m

    Breast milk or first infant formula only. No water, juice or solids needed. Vitamin D for breastfed babies (NHS: 8.5–10 µg a day; AAP: 400 IU).

  • Around 6 m

    Start solids alongside milk feeds, once the three readiness signs are there. Offer sips of water from a cup with meals.

  • 7–12 m

    Move to mashed, lumpy and finger foods, building up to three meals a day. Milk is still the main drink.

  • 12 m+

    Family foods (no added salt), three meals and 2–3 healthy snacks. Whole cow’s milk as a main drink is fine; breastfeeding can continue.

Fed is the goal: getting support

Feeding can be hard work at first. In the UK, ask your midwife or health visitor, call the National Breastfeeding Helpline (0300 100 0212) or find a local infant feeding team. In the US, ask your paediatrician for a lactation consultant (IBCLC) or contact La Leche League or WIC. If you use formula, your health visitor or paediatrician can help you choose and make up feeds safely.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know my baby is getting enough milk?
Plenty of wet and dirty nappies (at least 6 heavy wet nappies a day after the first week), steady weight gain after the first couple of weeks, and a baby who seems content after most feeds are good signs. Your midwife, health visitor or paediatrician will check weight.
Can I combine breast and bottle feeding?
Yes. Many families combine breast milk and formula (mixed or combination feeding). If you’re breastfeeding, getting support first can help protect your milk supply.
