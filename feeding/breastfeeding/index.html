Breastfeeding: a gentle start

Breastfeeding is natural, but it is also a skill you and your baby learn together. These basics, plus good support, make the early weeks easier.

Getting a good latch

  1. Hold your baby close, tummy to you, with head, shoulders and body in a line.
  2. Nose to nipple: start with your baby's nose level with your nipple so they tilt their head back.
  3. Wait for a wide mouth, then bring your baby to the breast (not breast to baby), chin first.
  4. Check: more of the darker skin is visible above the top lip than below, cheeks look full and round, and you hear swallowing after the first quick sucks.

Feeding shouldn't keep hurting. Some tenderness at first is common, but ongoing pain, cracked or bleeding nipples are a sign to get help with positioning and attachment.

How often do newborns breastfeed?

Often. In the early weeks, many babies feed 8 to 12 times or more in 24 hours, including at night, and feeds vary in length. Feeding responsively (whenever your baby shows hunger cues) helps build your supply, because milk production works on supply and demand.

Cluster feeding, when a baby feeds very frequently over a few hours (often in the evening), is normal and not a sign your milk is “running out”.

Common problems and what helps

ProblemWhat it can feel likeWhat often helps
EngorgementFull, hard, sore breasts in the first daysFeed often, hand express a little to soften, cold compresses after feeds
Sore or cracked nipplesPain during feeds, damaged skinCheck latch with a midwife or lactation consultant; nipple cream if needed
Blocked ductA tender lumpKeep feeding, gentle hand expression, rest; get help if it doesn’t clear
MastitisRed, hot, painful area, flu-like feelingsContact your GP, midwife or doctor the same day; you may need treatment
ThrushBurning or shooting pains, white patches in baby’s mouthSee your GP or doctor; you and your baby may both need treatment

Expressing, pumping and storing breast milk

You can express by hand or with a pump. NHS storage guidance: freshly expressed breast milk can be kept in the fridge at 4°C or lower for up to 8 days, in a freezer compartment of the fridge for up to 2 weeks, or in a freezer at -18°C or lower for up to 6 months. Thaw in the fridge and use straight away; never refreeze. US guidance from the CDC differs slightly, so follow your own health service's advice.

Look after yourself

Drink to thirst, eat regularly and rest when you can. Most medicines are compatible with breastfeeding, but always ask a pharmacist or doctor. The NHS and AAP advise against drinking alcohol in large amounts and smoking while breastfeeding. If breastfeeding isn't working for you, that's OK: speak to your midwife, health visitor or paediatrician about combination feeding or formula.

Frequently asked questions

How long should I breastfeed?
The WHO and NHS recommend exclusive breastfeeding for around the first 6 months, then continuing alongside solid foods for as long as you and your baby want. The WHO and AAP mention up to 2 years or beyond. Any amount of breast milk has benefits.
Is it normal for my baby to feed every hour?
In the early weeks and during growth spurts, frequent feeding and evening cluster feeds are common. If your baby has plenty of wet and dirty nappies and is gaining weight, it’s usually normal, but ask your midwife or health visitor if you’re worried.
Where can I get free breastfeeding help?
In the UK: your midwife or health visitor, the National Breastfeeding Helpline (0300 100 0212) and local infant feeding teams. In the US: your hospital or paediatrician’s lactation consultants, La Leche League and WIC.
