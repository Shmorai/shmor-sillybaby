Bronchiolitis in babies: what to look for, and when to get help

Winter coughs and snuffles are everywhere, and for most babies they stay mild. Bronchiolitis is the one to know about: a common chest infection in under-twos, usually caused by RSV, that can occasionally become serious. Here is what the NHS and CDC say about spotting it, caring for your baby and the protection now offered in pregnancy.

What is bronchiolitis?

Bronchiolitis is a common chest infection that mainly affects babies and children under 2. The NHS says it is usually mild but can be serious. It is caused by a virus, most often respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), which spreads easily through coughs and sneezes. Nearly all children catch RSV before they turn 2.

The CDC adds that RSV spreads in the autumn and winter and usually peaks in December and January in the US, and that it is the most common cause of bronchiolitis in children under 1. Babies under 6 months, premature babies and children with heart, lung or immune conditions are more likely to become seriously ill.

Bronchiolitis symptoms: how it starts and develops

It begins like a cold: sneezing, a runny or blocked nose and a slightly raised temperature. Over the next few days you may see:

  • a cough
  • faster breathing
  • difficulty feeding
  • noisy breathing, such as wheezing or crackling
  • more irritability than usual

The NHS says symptoms are usually worst around days 3 to 5, and the cough often clears within about 3 weeks. The CDC notes that in very young babies the only signs may be irritability, being less active and breathing difficulties, so a baby who seems "off" deserves a closer look.

When to call 999 (or 911) or get urgent advice

NHS guidance says to call 999 if your baby:

  • is having difficulty breathing: grunting, the tummy sucking in under the ribs, or very fast breathing
  • has pauses in their breathing
  • has blue or grey skin, lips or tongue (on brown or black skin, check the palms, soles, lips, gums and inside the eyelids)
  • is floppy and will not wake up or stay awake

Get urgent GP advice or call NHS 111 if a cold is getting worse, feeding is much reduced, there has been no wet nappy for 12 hours or more, or your baby under 3 months has a temperature of 38°C or higher (39°C or higher for 3 months and over). Our fever guide covers temperatures in more detail. In the US, the CDC advises contacting a healthcare professional if your child has difficulty breathing, is not drinking enough or is getting worse. In an emergency, call 911. Trust your instincts: if you think your baby is seriously unwell, seek help.

Caring for a baby with bronchiolitis at home

There is no specific treatment, and most children recover at home. The NHS suggests:

  • keep your baby upright when they are awake, which can ease breathing
  • offer smaller, more frequent feeds, breast or bottle, so they stay hydrated
  • check on them regularly, including overnight
  • keep the home smoke-free, because smoke raises the risk of severe illness
  • use children's paracetamol (from 2 months) or ibuprofen (from 3 months) only if they are uncomfortable, and never both at once; never give aspirin to under-16s

Sleep stays the same: on the back, on a firm flat surface, nothing in the cot. Don't prop baby up to sleep on cushions or in unsuitable products, even with a stuffy nose. Our safe sleep guide has the full checklist, and the winter baby care post covers keeping the room at a comfortable temperature.

How to lower the chance of RSV

  • Wash your hands and your baby's hands often, and clean toys and surfaces.
  • Don't share cups and utensils, and bin used tissues straight away.
  • Keep newborns away from people with colds or flu, especially if your baby was premature or has a health condition.
  • Never smoke around your baby.

Visitors at Christmas are welcome, but it is fine to ask anyone with symptoms to stay away, or to wash their hands before holding the baby.

RSV vaccine in pregnancy and infant antibodies

UK: the NHS offers a free RSV vaccine in every pregnancy from 28 weeks, usually around your 28-week appointment. It helps protect your baby for roughly their first 6 months, when they are most at risk. If you haven't been offered it, contact your maternity service or GP surgery.

US: the CDC says the maternal RSV vaccine is given at 32 to 36 weeks of pregnancy, during September through January in most of the country, and protects the baby for about 6 months after birth. Babies under 8 months whose mothers did not receive it are recommended an RSV antibody (nirsevimab or clesrovimab), usually shortly before RSV season or within a week of birth if born from October to March. Most babies need only one of the two options.

Ask your midwife, obstetrician or paediatrician what applies to you, as recommendations can change.

Frequently asked questions

How long does bronchiolitis last?
The NHS says symptoms are typically at their worst around days 3 to 5 and the cough usually improves within about 3 weeks.
Is bronchiolitis the same as RSV?
Not exactly. RSV is the virus; bronchiolitis is the chest infection it most often causes in babies. Other viruses can cause bronchiolitis too.
Can bronchiolitis be treated with antibiotics?
The NHS says there is no specific treatment and most children recover at home with rest, fluids and watchful care, because it is a viral infection.
Can I get the RSV vaccine if I had it in a previous pregnancy?
In the UK the NHS recommends it in every pregnancy. In the US, the CDC does not currently recommend another dose if you had it in a previous pregnancy; your baby should get an RSV antibody instead. Check with your clinician.
