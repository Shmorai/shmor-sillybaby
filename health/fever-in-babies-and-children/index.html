Fever in babies and children

A high temperature is very common in children and is usually the body fighting off an infection. Here is how to check it, what helps and when to get help fast.

What counts as a high temperature?

The NHS defines a high temperature in children as 38°C (100.4°F) or above. The AAP uses the same 100.4°F (38°C) threshold for babies.

AgeTemperatureWhat to do
Under 3 months38°C (100.4°F) or higherGet urgent medical advice: call your GP or NHS 111 (UK) or your paediatrician (US) straight away.
3 to 6 months39°C (102.2°F) or higherGet urgent medical advice (NHS).
Any ageHigh temperature with warning signs belowCall 999 / 911 / 112.
Older babies and childrenHigh temperature, otherwise copingFluids, rest, light clothing; get advice if it lasts over 5 days, they’re getting worse or you’re worried.

Call 999 (UK), 911 (US) or 112 (EU) if your child

  • has a stiff neck, or a rash that doesn't fade when you press a glass against it,
  • is bothered by light,
  • has a fit (seizure) for the first time, or one that lasts more than 5 minutes,
  • has unusually cold hands and feet with a high temperature,
  • has blue, grey, pale or blotchy skin, lips or tongue,
  • is floppy, unusually sleepy or hard to wake,
  • finds it hard to breathe, is grunting or their tummy sucks in under the ribs,
  • has a weak, high-pitched or continuous cry, or isn't responding as normal.

How to take a temperature

Use a digital thermometer. For babies under 5 the NHS recommends an under-the-arm (armpit) reading with a digital thermometer; ear (tympanic) thermometers are another option for older children. In the US, the AAP says a rectal reading is the most accurate for babies under 3 months; ask your paediatrician how to do it. Strip-type forehead thermometers aren't reliable.

Looking after a child with a fever

  • Fluids: offer plenty of drinks (breast milk or formula for babies). Watch for dehydration: fewer wet nappies, dry mouth, no tears, sunken soft spot.
  • Comfort: light clothing, a comfortable room. Don't undress them fully or sponge them with cold water.
  • Medicine: you can give paracetamol (acetaminophen) or ibuprofen if your child is distressed, following the dose on the pack for their age or weight. NHS: paracetamol from 2 months, ibuprofen from 3 months (if they weigh over 5kg). Don't give both at the same time, and ask a pharmacist before alternating. Never give aspirin to under-16s.
  • Check at night and keep them off nursery or school while unwell.

After vaccinations, a raised temperature is common. Ask your GP or nurse about paracetamol advice for your baby's vaccines.

Febrile seizures

Some young children (usually between 6 months and 3 years) have a fit when they have a high temperature, called a febrile seizure. They can be frightening but are usually not harmful. Keep your child safe on their side, time it, and call 999 / 911 if it's their first fit, it lasts more than 5 minutes, or they don't recover quickly.

Frequently asked questions

What temperature is a fever in a baby?
38°C (100.4°F) or higher. If your baby is under 3 months, get urgent medical advice at this temperature. For babies 3 to 6 months, get urgent advice at 39°C (102.2°F) or higher.
Should I wake my child to give medicine?
No need to wake a sleeping child just to give fever medicine; the aim is to make them comfortable, not to bring the number down. Do check on them during the night.
Can teething cause a high temperature?
The NHS says teething isn’t a cause of high temperature. If your baby has a fever, look for another cause and get advice if you’re worried.
How long does a fever last in children?
Most fevers from common viral infections settle within 2 to 3 days. The NHS advises getting medical advice if a high temperature lasts 5 days or more, or sooner if you’re worried.
